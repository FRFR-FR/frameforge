## 01 — THE INVISIBLE NETWORK

Every time you send a message across an ocean, something remarkable happens. A photograph leaves your phone, a video loads on another continent, or a bank payment crosses thousands of kilometres in less than a second. It feels like information simply disappears into the air. But much of the world's international connectivity depends on something far more physical: glass fibres resting on the ocean floor. They are submarine telecommunications cables, and they form one of the least visible parts of modern life. The International Telecommunication Union says these cables carry more than ninety-nine per cent of international data flows. That does not mean every internet connection is underwater. It means that when information has to cross national borders at scale, these hidden cables are usually doing the heavy lifting.

## 02 — A NETWORK UNDER THE SEA

The network is not one giant cable. It is a web of separate systems, built by different companies and consortia, linking landing stations on coastlines. From there, data moves through terrestrial fibre, data centres, routers and local networks before reaching a device. The scale is extraordinary. The ITU's 2025 Global Connectivity Report described more than five hundred operational cable systems and about one point four million kilometres of cable. The International Cable Protection Committee's more recent overview describes the global network as roughly one point eight million kilometres. These figures are snapshots with different publication dates and scopes, not a live odometer. The important point is the scale: the ocean floor has become a major part of the world's communications infrastructure.

## 03 — HOW LIGHT CARRIES INFORMATION

Inside the cable are optical fibres: extremely fine strands of glass engineered to guide light. A transmitter at one end converts electrical data into an optical signal. Lasers send that signal through the glass, and receivers at the other end turn it back into data that network equipment can use. The information is not simply one bright flash for every letter. Modern optical systems encode data using carefully controlled properties of light, allowing enormous amounts of information to travel together. The glass guides the light because of the optical properties of the fibre's core and surrounding cladding. The result is a long-distance communication path with very high capacity and low signal loss compared with many older transmission methods.

## 04 — KEEPING THE SIGNAL STRONG

Even in glass, a signal does not travel forever without weakening. Long-distance systems therefore use equipment called repeaters or optical amplifiers at intervals along the route. Their job is to restore the optical signal so it can continue across an ocean. These units are engineered to survive the deep-sea environment and are integrated into the cable system rather than being ordinary boxes that can be casually opened. The system also needs power. Electrical power is delivered from the landing stations through a conductor within the cable, feeding the submerged equipment. The International Telecommunication Union's technical recommendations describe how repeater housings, power-feeding components and optical amplifiers are designed as parts of one carefully engineered system.

## 05 — WHY THE CABLE IS BUILT IN LAYERS

A submarine cable is designed for its route, not for a single universal ocean-floor condition. At its centre are the optical fibres. Around them are protective and structural layers that may include strength members, insulation, water barriers and armour. The exact construction changes with the environment. Near shore, cables face more interaction with anchors, fishing gear and seabed activity, so additional protection and burial may be used. Farther out in deeper water, a lighter cable design can be appropriate because the risks are different. The ITU's 2025 recommendation describes several cable categories, from lightweight designs to single- and double-armoured constructions. The goal is to protect the fibres while keeping the system flexible and strong enough to be installed, recovered and repaired.

## 06 — HOW A CABLE REACHES THE SEABED

Before a cable is laid, engineers survey the seabed and plan a route that considers water depth, terrain, existing infrastructure and hazards. The route is not chosen by drawing a straight line between two cities. A specialist cable ship carries the cable in large tanks and pays it out at a controlled rate as the ship follows the planned path. In shallow areas, a sea plough or other submersible equipment may bury the cable to add protection. In deeper water, the cable may rest directly on the seabed. The ship must maintain careful control of speed, tension and the amount of cable released. The ITU's installation guidance also describes pre-lay route clearance, controlled cable overlength and testing during and after the laying operation.

## 07 — WHEN A CABLE NEEDS REPAIR

These systems are designed for long service lives, but faults still happen. The International Cable Protection Committee reports that roughly one hundred and fifty to two hundred submarine telecommunications cable faults occur worldwide each year. It says around seventy to eighty per cent are associated with accidental human activities, especially commercial fishing and ship anchors. Natural seabed movement, abrasion and equipment failures can also play a part. When a fault is detected, engineers use measurements and system monitoring to narrow down the affected section. A dedicated repair ship then travels to the area. Specialists recover the damaged cable, make a repair using additional cable and underwater joints, test the section, and carefully lower it back to the seabed. The exact procedure depends on depth and the type of fault.

## 08 — WHY ONE BREAK DOES NOT ALWAYS MEAN DISCONNECTION

The network is designed with routes and alternative paths, so traffic can sometimes be redirected when one system is unavailable. That does not make every place equally resilient. A country or island with only a small number of international connections may feel an outage more sharply than a region with many diverse routes. Congestion can rise, latency can change, and some services may become slower or temporarily unavailable. The ITU has warned that repair delays can be extended by permitting, customs and other logistical complications. Resilience therefore depends on more than the cable itself. It also depends on route diversity, landing-station capacity, terrestrial links, maintenance agreements and cooperation between countries and operators.

## 09 — THE WORLD BENEATH THE CONNECTION

The next time a video loads from another continent, remember that the connection is not magic. Somewhere beneath the waves, light is moving through carefully engineered glass, inside a cable laid by a specialist ship and connected to a network of landing stations and data centres. It is an extraordinary system precisely because most of us never see it. And it is a reminder that the digital world is built on physical infrastructure: ships, shore stations, power systems, glass fibres and thousands of kilometres of seabed routes. The internet may feel weightless. Its foundations are not. This is one of the hidden systems that keeps the modern world connected.
